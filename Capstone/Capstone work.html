§01

Abstract

This paper scores 91,587 content items across FlyRank's client warehouse into four states — growing, declining, recovering, or worth review — using a three-window impressions trajectory rather than a simple before/after comparison, so that pages which have already turned around are not mislabeled alongside pages still sliding.

An early version of this analysis reported that 53.1% of content was declining; before accepting that number, a completeness check on the trailing days of the warehouse uncovered a ~15% drop in reporting coverage in the most recent five days of data — an artifact, not a market shift. Anchoring the analysis window to the last date with full coverage revised the estimate to 44.9% declining, with recovering content nearly doubling from 9.1% to 14.6%.

The paper reports the corrected pipeline, four reason codes explaining roughly three-quarters of flagged declines, a per-client validation showing the pattern is directionally consistent but not uniform across FlyRank's clients, and a ranked, impact-weighted action list a search team could work from directly.

§02

Introduction & problem statement

A search team managing hundreds or thousands of content items cannot manually review each one every month. The Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring lane asks a narrower, answerable question: given recent search performance, which content items are growing, declining, recovering, or ambiguous enough to need a human look — and for the ones losing visibility, what does the evidence suggest is happening?

The goal is not a single accuracy number. It is a ranked, explainable action list: an output a search or content team could open on a Monday morning and start working from, with the highest-impact items first and a stated reason for each flag they can agree or disagree with. That framing shaped every methodology choice below — this paper favors interpretable rules over a black-box classifier, and spends real effort validating that the underlying signal is genuine before trusting it.

§03

Data

All analysis runs against the pseudonymized FlyRank/internship-warehouse release on Hugging Face, queried in place via DuckDB over Parquet — the raw warehouse is queried in place and is not redistributed. Two tables are used:

  • fact_content_daily_performance — daily Google Search Console impressions, clicks, and average position per content item per client.
  • fact_content_query_90d — a 90-day query-mix summary per content item (visible query count, rare/anonymized impression share, top-query concentration).

A data-completeness artifact, and how it was found

The first pass anchored all time windows on MAX(report_date) in the daily table — the most recent date present. A diagnostic count of distinct content items reported per day revealed a clear break: coverage held steady around 399,000–406,000 items per day through 2026-06-25, then dropped to roughly 345,000 items per day for the final five days in the table, while total impressions stayed roughly flat.

Why this matters

That pattern — total impressions steady, distinct content count falling — is the signature of an incomplete backfill, not a genuine traffic collapse: the same volume of search activity is being attributed to fewer reported items because long-tail items haven't finished landing in the warehouse for those trailing days. Any window built on MAX(report_date) would have those five thin days inside its last30 bucket, mechanically dragging every trailing-window comparison negative.

The fix: anchor all three windows on STABLE_CUTOFF = '2026-06-25', the last date with full-coverage reporting, rather than the table's true maximum date. Section 5 reports how much this changed the headline result.

Only content items with at least MIN_PRIOR_IMPRESSIONS = 100 impressions in both the prev30 and older30 windows are scored, to avoid classifying near-zero-traffic items where small denominators produce unstable percentage changes. This filter yields 91,587 content items with sufficient history across all three windows. No client names, domains, URLs, private queries, or credentials are used or reproduced anywhere in this paper.

§04

Methodology

Three-window trajectory, not a two-point comparison

Each content item's impressions are aggregated into three 30-day windows relative to STABLE_CUTOFF: last30 (most recent), prev30 (30–60 days back), and older30 (60–90 days back). A two-point comparison cannot distinguish a page that is still sliding from one that dropped and has since turned around — the three-point trajectory can, which is the difference between a declining label and a recovering one.

State classification

Percent change is computed for both the most recent transition (chg_recent = (last30 − prev30) / prev30) and the prior one (chg_prior = (prev30 − older30) / older30), then classified against symmetric ±15% thresholds:

  • growing — meaningful recent increase, not preceded by a prior decline reversal pattern.
  • declining — meaningful recent decrease, continuing a prior trend rather than reversing one.
  • recovering — recent increase following a prior decline: a genuine turnaround.
  • worth_review — insufficient history, a reversal from growth to decline, or a change too small to classify confidently. A deliberate "flag, don't guess" bucket rather than a forced binary call.

The ±15% threshold is a judgment call, chosen to exceed ordinary week-to-week noise without being so wide that only extreme movers get flagged. It is stated explicitly here so it can be challenged or tuned by whoever uses this pipeline next.

Reason codes

Every declining or worth_review item is additionally tagged with one or more descriptive reason codes computed from the observed performance windows. These codes explain the observed movement; they are not predictive features:

  • position_worsened — average SERP position dropped by ≥1 point.
  • ctr_drop_despite_stable_impressions — impressions held (≥90% of prior) but click-through rate fell >1pt, pointing at a title/meta/SERP-appearance issue rather than a visibility issue.
  • over_reliant_on_single_query — over 70% of an item's impressions come from one query, a concentration risk.
  • high_volatility_noisy_trend — day-level impression coefficient of variation ≥1.0 over the trailing 90 days; the "decline" may be noise around a flat average rather than a trend.
  • severe_broad_decline — a catch-all for drops ≥50% with none of the above signals present. Intentionally descriptive rather than diagnostic; see Limitations.

Validation across clients

A rule tuned against one or two large clients would not generalize. Clients (not rows) were split 50/50 using GroupShuffleSplit(random_state=42), so no client's content appears in both halves, and the state distribution was compared across the two groups — a fair test of whether the pattern is a warehouse-wide signal or an artifact of a few dominant accounts.

§06

Limitations & honest framing

  • Client concentration. The top 10 clients account for roughly 87% of all scored rows, and their individual declining shares range from about 31% to 79%. The warehouse-wide numbers reported above are meaningfully shaped by a small number of large accounts; they are not evidence of a uniform, industry-wide pattern.
  • Thresholds are a judgment call, not a derived optimum. ±15% and the volatility/position cutoffs were chosen to be defensible, not fit to any objective function. A different reasonable choice would shift the state boundaries somewhat; the qualitative story is more robust than any single percentage.
  • ~25% of flagged items remain unclassified. The current reason codes explain roughly three-quarters of declining/worth_review items. The remainder likely includes causes outside this analysis's feature set (external demand shifts, seasonality, competitor moves) and is reported as a gap rather than papered over.
  • severe_broad_decline is descriptive, not diagnostic. It is close to restating "this item declined a lot" rather than explaining why, and is labeled accordingly so it isn't mistaken for a causal finding.
  • Correlational, not causal. A position_worsened tag says position and impressions moved together — it cannot establish that a ranking change caused the impression change, or rule out a shared external cause for both.
  • Extreme percentage changes come from small denominators. The largest positive chg_recent values (multiple-hundred-percent increases) all occur on items with a low imp_prev30 base (roughly 100–300); none of these appear in the declining state, but they are a reminder that percentage-based thresholds behave differently near the MIN_PRIOR_IMPRESSIONS floor.
  • Single time period. This analysis reflects one 90-day window ending 2026-06-25. It has not been validated against a second period, so it cannot yet distinguish a stable pattern from one specific to this quarter.
§07

Ranked recommendations

An action playbook mapped from state and reason code to a concrete next step:

01
declining + position_worsened → ranking/technical review

The largest identified group (32.1% of flagged items). Prioritize by impressions_at_stake and route to a technical/authority review for further investigation.

02
ctr_drop_despite_stable_impressions → title/meta refresh

Small group but a clean, high-confidence signal: visibility held, clicks didn't. These are the cheapest wins in the list — a title/meta/snippet update, not a content rebuild.

03
over_reliant_on_single_query → diversify targeting

Content dependent on one query is fragile by construction. Recommend expanding on-page coverage of adjacent query intent before this item's single query drifts or gets contested.

04
high_volatility_noisy_trend → hold, don't act yet

These items look like they're declining but their day-level variance is high enough that the "decline" may be noise. Recommend a longer observation window before committing resources.

05
unclassified_decline / severe_broad_decline → manual review queue

No automated reason code fires with confidence for roughly a third of flagged items. Route to a human reviewer rather than force an automated label.

06
growing → protect, don't touch

Explicitly exclude growing items from any refresh/rewrite queue. A well-intentioned edit to a page that is already working is a common way well-meaning content teams cause self-inflicted declines.
